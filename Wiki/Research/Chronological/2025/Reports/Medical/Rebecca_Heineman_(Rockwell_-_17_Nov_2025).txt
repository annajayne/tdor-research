> Rebecca Heineman, 1st National Gaming Champ, Has Died at 62: Games "Allowed Me to Play as Female"
> Heineman, a trans trailblazer, has played important roles in the gaming space.
> November 19, 2025
> 
> A celebrated trans history-maker in the video gaming world has passed away.
> 
> On Monday it was announced that Rebecca Heineman, a video game programmer and reportedly first-ever national video game champion, had died at 62. Heidi McDonald, a friend of Heineman, broke the news on social media site 
> 
> “My trailblazing game industry bad-ass friend Rebecca Heineman has passed away,” McDonald wrote. “Fuck cancer.”
> 
> Heineman had been diagnosed with cancer shortly before her death, according to a GoFundMe she had created in October.
> 
> “I’m in pain. I’ve been diagnosed with adenocarcinoma a few weeks ago and I went from being an active outgoing computer nerd into a nearly bedridden cancer patient who is non stop exhausted,” Heineman wrote. “Insurance is helping, but some of my treatment are out of network so I’m getting huge bills.”
> 
> On November 16th, Heineman updated the GoFundMe to share that, according to her doctors, it was “time” and that any further treatments for her cancer were “pointless.” She asked for more donations which would go towards funeral costs.
> 
> Heineman became the first national video game champion ever in 1980, when she won a tournament for the game Space Invaders hosted by Atari, per Gayming Magazine. After her victory, Heineman, who was a self-taught programmer, went on to help develop several iconic video games, including The Bard’s Tale 3: Thief of Fate, Wasteland and Wolfenstein 3D. She was also cofounder of Interplay Entertainment, a studio that went on to create such games as Baldur’s Gate and Fallout, per Gayming Magazine. In addition, the trailblazer consulted with some of the biggest names in the gaming industry, including Electronic Arts, Gearbox, Amazon, Microsoft, and Ubisoft.
> 
> Throughout her career, Heineman also worked to make corporate gaming spaces more welcoming to LGBTQ+ people; she was the “Transgender Chair” of Amazon’s LGBTQ+ group, Glamazon and played a similar role at Microsoft’s GLEAM group. While at Sony, she joined the Sony Rainbow Coalition and became a board member at GLAAD, per Games Beat.
> 
> Heineman was able to open up about her experiences as part of the Netflix docuseries High Score, which told a variety of stories about the establishment of the video gaming industry. In the documentary, Heineman said that she played video games because they allowed her to explore her gender identity.
> 
> “It allowed me to be myself,” she said in High Score. “It allowed me to play as female.”
> 
> Heineman came out as trans in 2003 and married JennellJaquays, who was also a trans woman and major figure in the video game industry, in 2013. That same year, the pair cofounded Olde Sküül, the gaming company that Heineman served as the CEO for until her death. Jaquays died in 2024 and, in many of her final posts, Heineman made direct reference to Jaquays. In her GoFundMe, she asked for funeral funds so she can “make a worthy entrance for reuniting with my one true love, Jennell Jaquays.”
> 
> Heineman’s death inspired an outpour of love from those she inspired in the world of video games.
> 
> Atari, the gaming company that hosted the tournament that Heineman won in 1980, posted about her death on Tuesday.
> 
> “We join so many of our colleagues and peers today mourning the loss of Rebecca Heineman, one of the true pioneers of the industry,” the company wrote on social media. “Our thoughts are with Rebecca’s family.”
> 
> https://twitter.com/atari/status/1990816270715887716
> 
> “From all of us who worked with Becky at Interplay Entertainment, and the rest of us who have deep respect for her contribution to our industry, we want to extend our condolences to Rebecca Heineman's family and friends,” Obsidian wrote. “Her spirit will live on in all who learned from her and loved her.”
> 
> https://twitter.com/Obsidian/status/1990886520413360565
> 
> Video game designer Brian Fargo called her “one of the most brilliant programmers around” in a social media post on Monday. Fargo added that Heineman had messaged him a final goodbye before her passing, with her reportedly saying, “We have gone on so many adventures together! But, into the great unknown! I go first!!!”
> 
> https://twitter.com/brianfargo/status/1990561629080277499
> 
> Esports journalist Rod Breslau remembered Heineman, who he called an “icon,” in a post, saying she was “immeasurable inspiration.”
