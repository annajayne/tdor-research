> Fresh inquest ordered into death of trans man, Aiden Longmuir, following legal challenge
> 17 Mar 2026
>
> A fresh inquest will be held into the death of 20-year-old trans man Aiden Longmuir, after the first inquest was quashed following a legal challenge brought by his family.
> 
> Aiden died by suicide in May 2025. An inquest was held in October 2025 and Aiden’s family raised serious concerns about the circumstances leading to his death, including the lack of gender-affirming care he had received.
> 
> Aiden’s family was supported in their legal challenge by the Good Law Project and represented by law firm Leigh Day.
> 
> Aiden had been diagnosed with gender dysphoria in 2018 and had been on the waiting list for the Tavistock Gender Identity Clinic since 2021 without receiving any support.
> 
> The original inquest into Aiden’s death was listed as a documentary inquest, meaning no oral evidence was heard. The coroner ruled that many of the family’s concerns – including delays in accessing gender identity services, the lack of support from his GP, and failures by his local Trust in providing mental health care – were outside the scope of the inquest.
> 
> The coroner also recorded Aiden’s sex as “female” on the record of inquest and death certificate, rather than his lived gender of “male”. She said she was required to make this finding of fact following the Supreme Court’s decision in For Women Scotland. In their legal challenge, Aiden’s family argued this was legally incorrect and went against the Chief Coroner’s Guidance, which states:
> 
> “It is the Chief Coroner’s view that the law does not prevent a trans person’s death being registered in the deceased’s experienced or acquired gender (whether the person has or has not got a GRC [gender recognition certificate]).”
> 
> Aiden’s family argued that the coroner's misinterpretation of the law, combined with the refusal to adjourn the inquest and the failure to hold a pre-inquest review hearing, meant the inquest was unlawful. Aiden’s family also raised concerns about breaches of Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which protects the right to private and family life - and covers the dignity and identity of trans people, and the need to treat someone’s death with respect for the feelings of their family.
> 
> Following the family’s pre-action letter, the Assistant Coroner for Surrey accepted that several key decisions in the inquest into Aiden’s death were unlawful and agreed that the inquest should be quashed. On 3 March 2026, the High Court approved a consent order confirming that: The original inquest is formally quashed; a pre-inquest review hearing must take place within 12 weeks of the order; a new inquest must be held within six months, subject to witness availability; all matters required by s.5 of the Coroners and Justice Act 2009 will be considered afresh, including the gender recorded on Aiden’s death certificate.
> 
> Aiden’s family are supported by the Good Law Project and are represented by Leigh Day solicitors Kate Egerton and Ellie Fawcett, and Jude Bunting KC of Doughty Street Chambers and Rosalind Comyn of Matrix Chambers.
> 
> Aiden’s mother, Willow, said: “No family should have to experience the trauma and indignity that we have been through. We feel we have been deeply let down by the coronial process where matters we consider essential to understanding why Aiden is no longer with us were ignored.
> 
> :All we wanted was for Aiden to be treated with respect and for the circumstances of his death to be properly investigated. We hope that this can finally happen with a fresh inquest.”
> 
> Jo Maugham, Director and Founder at Good Law Project, said:  "A system that failed Aiden in life then failed him again in death. I have to say, this feels to me like a quite shockingly callous decision by the Coroner and I am pleased it will not stand,"
> 
> Kate Egerton, solicitor at Leigh Day, added: “This case highlights the importance of dignity and respect for trans individuals and ensuring that bereaved families are given a fair opportunity to participate in the inquest process. We welcome the decision to hold a new inquest and sincerely hope that Aiden’s family will finally be heard.”




> Aiden was just 20 when he died, less than 6 months ago in May 2025.
> 
> When I spoke to Aiden’s family and asked them what he was like, one of the first responses was that he always said he was an unlucky person, and it feels like that sometimes.
> 
> They remember Aiden as a very good soul, someone who was kind and considerate. Aiden loved animals, birds and his garden of wildflowers. He had a gecko called Paprika, a fishtank full of fish and a cat, Dusk.
> 
> Aiden’s favourite colour was purple, the deep dark Cadburys purple. Aiden had sensory difficulties, and there were some textures he didn’t like to touch, but with his mum’s help for chopping, he’d cook dinner, chicken stir fry noodles being a favourite. He was also partial to pizza, plain cheese or pepperoni and his real favourite meal was fish and chips.
> 
> Aiden liked computers and had a close circle of online friends, who reached out to his family after his death, and attended his funeral online. Aiden also had one or two close friends who were nearby, although Aiden found it increasingly difficult to leave his home. When he was able to though, he’d pop to the local coop store, knock on his friend’s downstairs window and leave them a bottle of coke. Aiden’s family describe him as very caring, deeply caring.
> 
> 
> The small things mattered to Aiden. He liked to gift handmade gifts as presents and he’d take as much care and time wrapping them beautifully, as making them.
> 
> When I ask what made Aiden laugh, his mum says her ineptitude with technology and her doing silly things, or Dusk, his cat doing silly things.
> 
> Aiden was diagnosed autistic when he was about 8. His family had already identified he was autistic, which didn’t come as a surprise as his mother is also autistic. They tell me that Aiden was extremely good at masking and he found life incredibly tough at times.
> 
> Aiden did not find school easy. He would get overwhelmed at primary school and was suspended multiple times. The day before Aiden left his primary school, despite them knowing of his difficulties and his diagnosis of autism, the school called his mum in and said that Aiden would not cope in secondary school, and they needed to do an EHCP (Education, Health and Care Plan).
> 
> Unsurprisingly that was not done by the time Aiden transferred to secondary school. When Aiden moved to secondary school he struggled to attend and he had a lot of time off. His mum was called in to speak with his form tutor. When she mentioned to Aiden’s form tutor that he was autistic, they said “that answers a lot of questions”. His form tutor had never been told.
> 
> About 5 or 6 months into his time at mainstream secondary, Aiden stopped attending. At this stage, Aiden had had his long hair cut off and was sporting a more masculine haircut, but he had not yet transitioned. After some time off school, Surrey County Council found him a place at Limpsfield Grange, a local special school for autistic girls.
> 
> Aiden had very long days to attend school, leaving the house at 07:15 and not getting back until after 5pm each evening.
> 
> He continued to struggle and found it difficult to attend school. One day he called his mum asking her to ring the school and let them know he was heading to the station, he’d left school and no-one had noticed. The school did not want him to go home, so his mum sent a taxi to collect him. Aiden never went back to that school.
> 
> His mum describes snatched hours of support here and there after that. Aiden never returned to formal education.
> 
> He would go out with people, an hour here or there for education.
> 
> The family had very mixed experiences of social services support and ultimately Aiden was left to make his own way in the world.
> 
> They tell me Aiden was very smart, enjoyed reading, writing and taking in information. At the time of his death Aiden had been teaching himself Korean and Russian with the help of online resources, his mum says he liked the challenge.
> 
> I asked what Aiden wanted to be when he grew up and his family members (I’m speaking to his biological mother Willow, and his adoptive mother Amanda, who is Willow’s mum) respond in unison:
> 
> To be a boy, a proper male. Other than that, his other wish was to get his head sorted. He didn’t ask for much.
> 
> Aiden asked to be known as Aiden when he left Limpsfield Grange. He left his mum a note on the side saying that he wanted to be called Aiden. She tells me Aiden was on a call with a friend online when she saw the note. She went upstairs and asked what it meant and he asked if she’d speak with his friend who’d explain, because he was worried what her reaction would be, because some families had turned against some of his friends.
> 
> Aiden’s family supported him, always. In 2021, when Aiden was 16, his GP referred him to the Gender Identity Clinic (GIC) at The Tavistock. His GP accepted him into the gender pathway but said they were limited in what care they could provide him without working in partnership with a gender identity clinic.
> 
> Aiden waited and waited. Aiden’s family consider he remained without appropriate medical or psychological support from that point until his death.
> 
> Aiden spent his time doing charity work. He would crochet for charities. He would make neonatal hats that he sent off to hospitals. He made bonding squares for premature babies and their families. He also made bereavement hearts and butterflies, used at the end of life, so one is in the hand of the person dying, and the other with their loved ones.
> 
> Helping people always made him happy.
> 
> Aiden received no transition to adult social care, despite him being open to Child and Adolescent Mental Health Services. Aiden’s family tell me that his autism was often blamed for his poor mental health, with one professional suggesting depression was an inherent aspect of being autistic.
> 
> Aiden was fearful he might spend years on a waiting list for a Gender Identity Clinic, only to then be told that he couldn’t be helped because he was autistic.
>
> There was so much unknown.
> 
> Aiden turned 18 in April 2023, while still waiting for an appointment. His mental health was suffering but his family tell me he received no support at all.
> 
> Aiden’s GP made a re-referral to the GIC once Aiden turned 18. They heard nothing back. In February 2025 Aiden attended an appointment with his GP requesting they prescribe him testosterone. The GP said they were unable to do so without agreement from a GIC, but despite being on the waiting list for 4 years Aiden had still not heard anything from them.
> 
> Aiden’s health deteriorated. Towards the end of February, the GP contacted Aiden and spoke to his mum, explaining that the GIC had said they’d honour the 2021 referral, but by the time of Aiden’s death 2.5 months later, he’d still not heard anything from them.
> 
> Aiden died at the start of May. His cause of death will be ascertained at his inquest this afternoon.
> 
> The coroner is holding a documentary inquest, that is matters are being decided without any oral evidence.
> 
> His family tell me they were told a week ago that it would happen today, and they have asked for it to be adjourned, to allow them time to seek legal representation, and because they think it needs more exploration than a documentary inquest will allow.
> 
> The coroner has decided to proceed.
> 
> I’ll be driving home after court today and will share what happened over the weekend/at the start of next week.
> 
> I’ll give the final words here to Aiden’s family.
> 
> All he ever wanted to do was help other people and be accepted, he really, really wanted to be accepted … be more kind, if it’s not hurting you just walk on.



> Aiden Longmuir’s family fought hard for his death certificate to reflect his identity as a man – we’re helping them make sure the coroner gives him the respect he deserves
> 
> When a trans man died by suicide, the coroner issued a death certificate that said he was a woman. Following legal action from Good Law Project, there will be a new inquest – with a new coroner.
> 
> Aiden Longmuir took his own life in May 2025, when he was 20 years old. He had been living as a man since he was 12, and appeared as male in all his medical and financial records.
> 
> Three days after he died, Woking Coroner’s Court got in touch with Aiden’s family about an inquest into his death. The family asked for his death certificate to list him as a man, and were told that this would be at the coroner’s discretion.
> 
> Despite repeated attempts to find out what the coroner would decide, when the assistant coroner opened the hearing on 24 October, the family still didn’t know how this vital question would be resolved.
> 
> At the hearing, the coroner said that she had to make “findings of fact for the purpose of registration”. Citing the Supreme Court decision on the definition of sex under the Equality Act, she found that “biologically the body was that of a female”.
> 
> But the law governing the registration of deaths doesn’t define “sex”. And the Supreme Court judgment doesn’t say anything about how sex should be defined on death certificates.
> 
> The coroner’s decision also runs directly against guidance the chief coroner issued in June 2025, which says that “if after making enquiries a coroner concludes a person has chosen to present as male then the coroner can regard them as male”. It would be clear, even in the most basic of enquiries, that Aiden was presenting and living as male. 
> 
> When the family stood up to the coroner, quoting this guidance, she said that it was “at odds with how I interpret the law”.
> 
> Even though the family told her how hard Aiden fought to be accepted, and that recording him as a woman would be “like spitting in his face”, the coroner issued a death certificate calling him “female”.
> 
> When the family reached out to Good Law Project for help, we worked with Leigh Day to prepare a legal challenge to this decision. We wrote to Surrey County Council setting out the law around registering deaths, explaining how this decision could be in breach of Aiden’s right to a private life, and laying out other issues with the inquest that could make it unlawful.
> 
> The coroner has now agreed to quash the inquest, saying that a new inquest will be held before a different coroner. This inquest will consider Aiden’s’s death again, including the question of how he should be recorded on his death certificate.
> 
> According to Leigh Day’s Kate Egerton, this case “highlights the importance of dignity and respect for trans individuals”.
> 
> “We welcome the decision to hold a new inquest,” Egerton said, “and sincerely hope that Aiden’s family will finally be heard.”
> 
> For Cat Knight, deputy head of legal at Good Law Project, this case shows how we can “provide a voice for trans people when they can no longer be heard”.
> 
> “No family should have to battle for their loved one to be officially recorded in death as they were in life,” Knight said. “Ignoring a family’s wishes distracts from the important work of finding out how someone died, and only adds to the trauma of their darkest hour.”
> 
> Good Law Project will support the family with the costs of the new inquest and will be following it very closely. And if coroners fail to respect trans people, we won’t fail to challenge them.
