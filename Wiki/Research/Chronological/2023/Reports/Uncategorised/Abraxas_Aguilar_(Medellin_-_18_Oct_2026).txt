> We deeply regret the passing of Abraxas, a trans woman, visual artist, collector, and collage expert, who for over 40 years offered a welcoming space for LGBTIQ+ people in Medellín. 
> 
> We honor her life and her legacy of trans art and resistance.


> The artist, lawyer, and activist Abraxas Aguilar died in the early hours of Sunday, October 18, as confirmed to EL COLOMBIANO by friends and close acquaintances. She was suffering from a terminal illness and was hospitalized.
> 
> A trans woman born in Medellín, Aguilar dedicated more than four decades to collage and turned her downtown home into a museum.
> 
> In 2024, media outlets reported that the space, located at Calle 57 # 50A-3, contained approximately 100,000 images of floors, walls, and ceilings , and that it had been declared a cultural heritage site of Medellín.
> 
> His work earned him two Guinness World Records in 1997. One, for the world's largest collage book, which measures two meters, weighs about half a ton and has 200 pages.
> 
> The other, for a collage of 20,000 images, the largest number recorded at the time for a piece of that type.
> 
> She took her name from Abraxas, the ancient god who reconciled opposites. In addition to being a visual artist, she was a poet, playwright, lawyer, and cultural manager.
> 
> Her time in politics also shaped her life. She was a councilwoman in Medellín in the 1970s and ran for mayor of Medellín, governor of Antioquia, and president of Colombia. Over the years, she left politics and opened her home to visitors.
> 
> Aguilar's passion for collage began in 1982, in the house where she lived at the time, in Villa Hermosa. "I used to fill my notebooks with these images," she told Voice of America in 2024.
> 
> Over time, the cut-out and pasted images came to cover more than 450 square meters of walls and ceilings. Every inch of wall was covered, and hundreds more collages piled up on tables and chairs.
>

> 
> Some pieces were turned into paintings by other painters, and others incorporated fragments of paintings given to him by his friends.



> Abraxas Aguilar
> He can be seen walking all over the city center, one day between La Paz Street or La Playa Avenue; the next, El Palo or Junín. Under his arm he carries magazines, books, and encyclopedias. Life condensed into images and words. He carries what will become the clippings that tell stories and that tell us stories, his meticulous selection of history.
> 
> "I live in my body. For me, downtown Medellín means saving time and money. I have the entire Aburrá Valley half a block away..."
> 
> But Medellín has changed; it no longer has the culture of 60 years ago. Only memories remain, so the focus for me, at a certain point, is to see the transmutation, the transformation, the transculturation, since I am very trans myself.”
> 
> She has been a collage artist for almost 35 years; she has transformed herself and her house, which has become a work of art. Two cats, Luz and Doble Cero, guard and accompany Abraxas as she glues each cutout in the place she carefully assigns to the unexpected meaning created in the collage.
