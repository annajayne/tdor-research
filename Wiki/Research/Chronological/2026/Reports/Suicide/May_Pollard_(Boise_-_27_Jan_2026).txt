> Idaho didn’t want May Pollard to live. Now she’s dead by her own hand.
> July 20, 2026 by Evan Urquhart
> 
> Before she took her life, May Pollard was a trans girl living in a torture box devised by the Republican party of Idaho. As reported by Jo Yurcaba in this devastating piece for NBC, the young plaintiff whose death brought an end to the legal challenge to Idaho’s bathroom ban died in January at the age of 16. Pollard had friends who cared and worried about her and wanted her to live. But Idaho Republicans worked tirelessly to ensure that she was not allowed a single day of peace.
> 
> Pollard wrote poetry and planned to study psychology at community college. She took care with her appearance, developing a style her family described as goth cottagecore. She played video games with friends online. She was, in other words, a perfectly normal teenage girl. But unlike other girls the stresses and strains of adolescence were dwarfed by the malign influence of politicians who began to dictate every waking moment of her life. 
> 
> In 2023, Idaho passed a law banning transgender students from using restrooms appropriate to the gender in which they lived. Pollard would have been about 13 then. Going by the timeline sketched out in Yurcaba’s story, the bathroom ban came into effect around the same time Pollard was coming out to her supportive parents, Joseph and Elyse, who are divorced. She lived her whole life as a trans girl under the bathroom ban, and under the Supreme Court validated ban on gender-affirming care, which required her mother to drive her 13 hours round trip twice a year to access the medical treatment her family and doctors felt she needed, care her mother told Yurcaba had brightened her and gave her some reprieve from the suicidal thoughts she had been struggling with.
> 
> The reprieve affirming care offered was not enough. As a trans girl in an Idaho school Pollard would never know a day without fear. According to NBC. she stopped eating or drinking anything during the school day because her only options to relieve herself would have outed her as trans.
> 
> “I worry about people gossiping and speculating about me being transgender,” she wrote in an affidavit to the court. “I have heard students refer to the single restroom in the main gym building as the “trans bathroom.”
> 
> The NBC story contains the caveats any responsible reporting must contain. Suicide is complex, and no single event or factor can be definitively said to be its cause. Simply being forced by political malevolence to live inside a torture box with escape can’t possibly be said to have caused this teenager girl to see death as preferable to remaining trapped. Maybe we should blame it on the rain.
> 
> We know May Pollard’s story because she was the plaintiff in a lawsuit that was withdrawn because there were no longer enough trans children left in Idaho public schools for lawyers to defend. Her story took two months to make it to the public, and if it were not for Yurcaba it may have gone untold. Even as “the trans debate” dominates national politics, somehow the consequences of anti-trans legislation on trans people’s lives have barely merited media attention at all.
> 
> There are untold teenagers in Pollard’s position, desperately trying to persevere even as the full weight of their government attempts to break them and drive them to despair. There are even more teenagers whose families have been poisoned by propaganda, unable to love their children as they are. How many teenagers like Pollard have taken their lives without the media taking any note at all? How many teenage girls like Pollard have had their deaths recorded as the death of a boy, laid to rest in suits and ties by families whose ideological poisoning closed off any hope of their being seen and loved for who they really were?
> 
> In 2024 Nature published a study showing that anti-trans legislation increases suicide attempts among trans youth, and still there’s been no hesitation, no sign of turning back. These laws are coming for our kids, seeking to destroy them for reasons of ideological rigidity and political gain. As we mourn Pollard, seven months late and with a name we only know by luck, the trans community also mourns the many trans girls like her whose names we’ll never know.



> Parents speak out after their teen died by suicide: Idaho’s anti-trans extremism took a toll
> When asked if anyone wanted to harm her daughter, her mother said “The whole f**king country” did.
> 
> The parents of a transgender teen who died by suicide earlier this year are speaking out about the toll Idaho’s anti-trans laws and the Trump administration’s anti-trans crusade took on their daughter.
> 
> “You worry about that hatred being internalized, and that’s what happened to my kid,” Elyse Thorpe told NBC News in a recent profile. “It got inside.”
> 
> Thorpe and ex-husband Joseph Pollard describe their late daughter, May Pollard, as an energetic and curious child who loved sloths, video games, and writing poetry. She just wanted to live her life and experience the same things any teen does. But she was also keenly aware of the wave of anti-trans laws and sentiment that have swept Idaho and much of the U.S. in recent years.
> 
> According to NBC News, May, who had also been diagnosed with autism, came out as trans when she was in eighth grade. She began receiving gender-affirming medical care in ninth grade. But due to Idaho’s ban on such care for minors, passed in 2022 and upheld by the Supreme Court in 2024, her parents were forced to travel to Portland, Oregon, every six months to get May the care she needed. Following President Donald Trump’s return to office in January 2025, Thorpe began making the 13-hour round trip by car to avoid creating a flight record amid the administration’s relentless crackdown on gender-affirming care for young people.
> 
> While the treatment improved things for May, the obstacles placed between her and the care she needed by anti-trans legislators continued to weigh on her.
> 
> Then, in July 2023, Idaho’s law banning trans students from accessing single-sex bathrooms and locker rooms that align with their gender identity took effect. Lambda Legal filed a lawsuit challenging the law on behalf of Boise High School’s Sexuality and Gender Alliance, and in July 2025, May joined the lawsuit under the pseudonym Jane Doe. In court filings, May described feeling stigmatized by being forced to use her campus’s two single-occupancy restrooms, which came to be referred to as the “trans bathrooms.”
> 
> “It is scary having to look around before to see if anyone will see me going into the single-user restroom, as I worry about people gossiping and speculating about me being transgender,” she said. “I don’t want people to know I am transgender without my consent — even students who might be friendly. For me, it is not a part of myself I talk about or that I feel is the most important part of my identity.”
> 
> Her parents told NBC News that May would avoid eating and drinking during the school day, hoping to not have to use the school’s bathrooms. “I just want to fit in,” she wrote in her affidavit in the Lambda Legal case. “It is upsetting to think that I may have to go through the rest of high school without access to something as basic as the ability to use the same restroom as everyone else.”
> 
> May’s parents say she became more troubled last fall and winter, according to NBC News. She attempted to run away to Oregon, and at least one of her poems seemed to reference self-harm. Then on the morning of January 27, Thorpe woke to find May missing from her bedroom. She later found her daughter’s body in a downstairs bedroom. May was just 16 years old.
> 
> When a detective asked her parents whether anyone might have wanted to harm May, Thorpe responded that “A lot of America” did.
> 
> “There is a huge amount of animosity, every single day, thrown at trans kids,” an angry and grieving Pollard told the detective. “The whole f**king country. The whole f**king administration, every single f**king day.”
> 
> As NBC News notes, over the last few years, Idaho Gov. Brad Little (R) has signed 15 anti-LGBTQ+ laws. In April, Little signed a pair of bills, one of which made it a criminal offense for trans people to use the bathroom associated with their gender, while the other banned Pride flags on government buildings.
> 
> As for Lambda Legal’s challenge to the state’s school bathroom ban, at the time of May’s death, she was one of just two plaintiffs left in the case, as all the others had graduated high school and no longer held standing. Earlier this spring, Lambda Legal moved to dismiss the case after the final plaintiff graduated.
> 
> But Thorpe has continued to protest Idaho’s anti-trans laws. According to NBC News, she stood outside Gov. Little’s office during a March 31 protest holding a photo of her daughter.
> 
> “I wanted to support the protesters and remind everyone, including the governor,” Thorpe told the outlet, “that the stakes are real.”
> 
> “The parents go and testify, and we say, ‘Children are going to die,’” she said. “We tell the legislators that, and they dismiss it. I guess they think it’s hyperbole, or they think it’s hysteria, or they think it’s an acceptable outcome, probably some mix of those things. So I don’t understand why there is an unwillingness to connect these laws to these outcomes. It was right there from the start.”

> A trans teen died by suicide in Idaho. Her family spoke out about state’s culture
> By Becca Savransky
> 
> The family of a transgender teenager in Idaho who was involved in a lawsuit over the state’s school bathroom law is speaking out after their 16-year-old daughter died by suicide in January.
> 
> The family spoke to NBC News about how the state’s laws targeting transgender people impacted their daughter on a daily basis, making it difficult for her to use the bathroom at school and to receive gender-affirming care.
> 
> “There is a huge amount of animosity, every single day, thrown at trans kids,” Joseph Pollard, May Pollard’s father, told a police detective after his daughter’s death, according to NBC News. “The whole f-----g country. The whole f-----g administration, every single f-----g day.”
> 
> The Idaho Legislature in 2023 passed a bill requiring public schools to have separate bathrooms, locker rooms and overnight accommodations for students based on their sex at birth. It also mandated that schools provide accommodations for students who are “unwilling or unable” to use multi-occupancy restrooms or changing facilities that align with their sex at birth.
> 
> Shortly after, opponents sued and asked the court for a preliminary injunction. They argued the school bathroom law violated the Constitution and would cause irreparable harm to transgender students across the state. In August 2025, the district court said the state “had a legitimate interest” in protecting privacy and that the plaintiffs were unlikely to succeed on constitutional claims. It also argued if the law did cause harm, it was “not irreparable because the law requires an alternative accommodation … .”
> 
> Earlier this year, the parties involved in the suit filed a stipulation for voluntary dismissal in the case. The motion came after May Pollard, who was identified as Jane Doe in the lawsuit, died by suicide, and the other student involved in the case graduated.
> 
> At the time, Idaho Attorney General Raúl Labrador sent a news release celebrating the outcome, with no mention of the student’s death. In response to a question from the Idaho Statesman, Labrador shared his thoughts for the child’s family, called it a “personal tragedy” and said his office doesn’t “comment on the private circumstances of individuals involved in litigation.”
> 
> Idaho in recent years has passed a host of laws targeting transgender people. Idaho has barred women and girls from participating in women’s and girls sports, restricted gender-affirming care for minors and, most recently, made it a crime for transgender people to use restrooms or changing rooms that align with their gender identity in most public spaces.
> 
> When she was in ninth grade, May told her dad she was having suicidal thoughts, and expressed her need to transition, her family told NBC. Her parents took her out of state to receive gender-affirming care after receiving a referral from their pediatrician for a doctor in Oregon, and spending months on a waitlist.
> 
> At school, May was able to use a restroom in the nurse’s office, or in another building across campus — options that she said felt alienating, she wrote in an affidavit.
> 
> “While I am transgender, I don’t view it as the defining feature of who I am as a person,” May wrote in the affidavit. “I just want to fit in. It is upsetting to think that I may have to go through the rest of high school without access to something as basic as the ability to use the same restroom as everyone else.”
> 
> Elyse Thorpe, May’s mother, participated in a sit-in outside Gov. Brad Little’s office in April after the governor signed the state’s new bathroom law. She held a photo of May. She said parents have warned the Legislature about the potential impacts of their decisions.
> 
> “The parents go and testify, and we say, ‘Children are going to die,’ ” Thorpe told NBC News. “We tell the legislators that, and they dismiss it. I guess they think it’s hyperbole, or they think it’s hysteria, or they think it’s an acceptable outcome, probably some mix of those things. So I don’t understand why there is an unwillingness to connect these laws to these outcomes. It was right there from the start.”
