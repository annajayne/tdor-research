> Yoisi Villalta, a transgender woman who worked as a pie vendor in Texistepeque, northern El Salvador, was found dead eight days after being reported missing. 
> 
> Her body was located in Tacuba, in the department of Ahuachapán. So far, the National Civil Police have not publicly released the cause of death nor classified the case as homicide or a hate crime. 
> 
> Therefore, it is not possible to say whether Yoisi was murdered because she was a transgender woman. Even so, her death has reignited denunciations of the violence and discrimination faced by the LGBTQ+ population in the country. 
> 
> During the search, organizations and people close to her denounced that Yoisi had been identified by the name on her documents, and not by the name she lived with and was socially recognized by. 
> 
> The Salvadoran LGBTI Federation requested that her memory be preserved "for her life, her work, her generosity and, above all, for her name." 
> 
> Before becoming news, Yoisi had a well-known presence in the community. In addition to working selling food, she collaborated with local sexual health initiatives, offering food to teams providing services in the region. 
> 
> This case occurs against a backdrop of serious allegations of violations against LGBTQ+ people in El Salvador. 
> 
> A survey by the ASPIDH LGBTQ+ Human Rights Observatory recorded 301 complaints between January and September 22, 2025. Transgender women represented 53.5% of the victims, while gay men accounted for 26.6%. 
> 
> The same survey indicated that discrimination, threats, and physical assaults were among the most frequent violations, and that public officials, including police officers, municipal agents, military personnel, and healthcare professionals, appeared among the groups denounced. 
> 
> El Salvador also still lacks a general gender identity law that would allow transgender people to adapt their documents to the identity with which they live.



> Salvadoran trans woman found dead
> The death of Yoisi Villalta brings the issue of violence back into the spotlight.
> September 8, 2026
> 
> SANTA ANA, El Salvador — The death of Yoisi Villalta, a trans woman and cake vendor in Texistepeque, Santa Ana Norte, has once again brought to the forefront a reality that has plagued the LGBTQ population in El Salvador for years: violence doesn't end with an attack or a death. It can also manifest itself in the way a person is searched for, named, registered, mourned, and remembered.
> 
> Villalta was reported missing by her family in late August 2026. Days later, news outlets and digital platforms reported the discovery of her body. A report published on September 5 indicates that the body was found in Tacuba, Ahuachapán, eight days after her disappearance, while the National Civil Police had not yet confirmed the cause of death or officially classified the case as intentional homicide.
> 
> Accuracy is important; at this time, it should not be stated as fact that Yoisi was the victim of a hate crime or a homicide motivated by her gender identity, until there is an official investigation that determines the circumstances and cause of her death. What does exist, however, is a deep concern expressed by civil society organizations and members of the LGBTQ community, especially given the context of violence and discrimination that trans women have historically faced in El Salvador.
> 
> Before becoming news, Villalta had a life. She was a trans woman who worked as a cake vendor in Texistepeque. From her business, she carried out a daily activity to earn a living, but she had also transformed that space into an expression of solidarity with her community.
> 
> According to information released by LGBTQ organizations, Villalta shared food with the sexual health brigades that conducted outreach in the area. This aspect of her life allows us to see her beyond the circumstances of her death: as a hardworking, compassionate woman deeply involved in community action.
> 
> The Salvadoran LGBTI Federation specifically requested that her memory be approached from that perspective. In the message released after her death, the organization called for her to be remembered for “her life, her work, her generosity, and above all, for her name,” and asked that her story not be reduced to sensationalism.
> 
> This appeal is especially important at a time when social media can transform a human tragedy into a barrage of insensitive comments, photos, speculation, and posts. A deceased person does not lose their dignity.
> 
> And a trans woman does not cease to be a woman because she has died.
> 
> One of the aspects that has provoked the most outrage surrounding the case is the handling of Villalta's identity during the search. Organizations and people close to her have denounced that during the search process, the name that appears on her National Identity Document (DUI) was used, instead of the name by which Villalta lived and was socially recognized.
> 
> This is not merely a semantic issue. For transgender people, a name can represent a fundamental part of their identity and how they construct their lives within society. Being recognized by the name they identify with is also a form of respect.
> 
> In Villalta's case, the Salvadoran LGBTI Federation stated that denying a trans person's name and identity also constitutes a form of violence. The situation is even more complex in El Salvador, where there is still no general legal mechanism allowing trans people to change their name and gender marker on their identity documents to reflect their gender identity.
> 
> Thus, a person can build a social, family, work, and community life for years under a particular name and yet find themselves facing institutions that continue to identify them exclusively through registry data that does not correspond to the identity they live with. The Villalta case again highlights this tension between lived identity and institutional recognition.
> 
> Villalta's case occurs in a country where human rights organizations have warned for years about the violence and discrimination affecting the LGBTQ population. The available information also reveals a problem with the documentation of these incidents.
> 
> Human rights organizations have questioned the absence or inadequacy of updated and disaggregated public records that would allow for a precise understanding of how many violent crimes affect LGBTQ people and how many of these crimes are potentially motivated by sexual orientation, gender identity, or gender expression. The lack of information does not necessarily mean the absence of violence. On the contrary, it can make it more difficult to identify.
> 
> A crime that fails to record the victim's gender identity, or that does not adequately investigate a possible bias-related motivation, ends up being statistically recorded as an isolated incident, preventing the identification of patterns.
