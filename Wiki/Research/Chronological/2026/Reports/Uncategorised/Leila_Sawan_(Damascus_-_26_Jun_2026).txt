====== Leila Sawan (Damascus - 26 Jun 2026) ======

> Leila Sawan was an energetic and vibrant 26-year-old Syrian trans woman. She loved to dye her hair red, a deep plum color, and had green-brown eyes that caught the sunlight when she laughed, which was often. She was like a little sister to me, and every week – and sometimes every day – for five years, she and I would exchange endless banter about politics, music, recipes, and our daily lives.
> 
> In the last week of June, Leila was found dead in her apartment in Damascus after returning to Syria the first time in 13 years. The circumstances of her death are unclear. Those of us that knew Leila knew that in the last few months of her life, she was the happiest she had ever been.
> 
> The death announcement in Damascus circulated her deadname, the name she hated, and she has been remembered in a way that ignored the visionary work she did around the world and everything she fought for to do it. This feels to me like an insult to her memory.
> 
> I write the following to remember the Leila I knew. Here is what I remember about Leila’s life.
> 
> A Childhood Shaped by Dictatorship and Displacement
> Leila was originally from the suburbs of Damascus. She once told me that some of her happiest childhood memories were of summers in Latakia, where her family would rent a chalet. But when she was eight, the Assad regime bulldozed her childhood home in front of her to build a highway and confiscated half their land. Her family was displaced, and the experience left her with lasting trauma.
> 
> Five years later, when Leila was thirteen, her neighbourhood in Jobar was destroyed by shelling by the former Syrian regime as the war escalated. One morning in 2013, on a Facebook album of massacre victims, she saw the dead bodies of several of her relatives. Her family was displaced a second time and fled to Jordan.
> 
> Displacement was one of the things that shaped Leila’s hatred of authoritarianism. Another was her relatives who were imprisoned by the Assad regime: her cousin in Adra prison for five years, and her uncle in al-Jawweh prison twice. When the regime fell, she searched for her family members in lists from Sednaya, hoping to find answers for the many disappeared her family had lost.
> 
> She was almost arrested twice as a child in Syria: once as she was walking to her aunt’s house in Abbaseen, and once at a checkpoint when she was thirteen years old. She lived, like most Syrian children in the suburbs of Damascus during the siege, a life of fear and surveillance. She critiqued authoritarianism everywhere, and later in life she would write about the connection between familial abuse and the patriarchal surveillance of the Assad regime, and about the hypocrisy of U.S. imperialism during the genocide in Gaza.
> 
> 
> A message Leila sent to me about being Syrian
> Leila and I shared many tastes and obsessions. We loved talking about emo and goth Syrian subcultures, and about Syrian goddesses, including Ishtar, who could be read through a trans lens. We talked about the Kurgarra and Galatur, trans-adjacent priestesses of ancient Sumeria. Leila was passionate about reclaiming indigenous histories, and about the complexities of Arabic linguistics and its roots as a source of knowledge. She once invited me to speak at a seminar she had organized about trans histories in Levantine goddess cosmologies.
> 
> We shared a love of Mashrou’ Leila and Hamed Sinno, and we were both devastated to learn of the death of Sarah Hegazi. Sarah was an Egyptian queer feminist and activist who became a symbol of Arab queer resistance after she raised a rainbow flag at a Mashrou’ Leila concert in Cairo in 2017. In the ensuing crackdown, she was arrested, imprisoned for three months and tortured in detention. After her release, she sought asylum in Canada, and tragically died by suicide in Toronto in June 2020, famously writing, “To the world – you were cruel to a great extent, but I forgive.”
> 
> Sarah’s death was particularly heartbreaking because she was a symbol of resilience for so many of us trying to find ourselves within communities that had displaced us and rejected us. In Leila’s case, that rejection began with her own family. She saw herself in Sarah’s story.
> 
> During quarantine, Leila’s father kicked her out of the house. He later bought her a vacation as an apology, saying: “It’s been a stressful year for you and you’re clearly confused and need to clear your mind, we’ll talk once you’re back.”
> 
> In 2021, her parents objected to her long hair, and she was forced to choose between cutting it and leaving home. Keeping her plum-colored hair long was the only way to alleviate her gender dysphoria. When her family threatened to cut it while she was sleeping, Leila chose to leave.
> 
> After leaving home, she stayed with a friend and had almost no money to feed herself. She went days without eating, sometimes surviving on a loaf of bread for days at a time. The malnutrition affected her so severely that she could barely function. That summer, she began gender-affirming hormone therapy – before hormones, she said, all she knew was depression. Hormones affirmed who she was, and she felt so much better.
> 
> In September 2021, Leila came out publicly as trans to her family. Her mother sent her a voicemail saying that she had three children instead of four and threatening to tell the community that Leila had died in a car accident. For Leila, the rejection was so excruciating that she could not even listen to the voicemail all the way through.
> 
> Mere months later, she went to a protest for queer and trans rights in Istanbul. She proudly held the trans flag high. She and her friends were attacked with tear gas. Leila told her friends, “We will not let them win,” and returned to the protest with her eyes streaming.
> 
> Surviving Crisis After Crisis
> One of the darkest periods of Leila’s life was the winter of 2021. Financial instability, hunger, and the rejection she experienced from her parents triggered a severe mental health crisis. She bravely checked herself into a mental hospital in Istanbul.
> 
> When she sent the bill to her family’s home, her father responded, “Are you trying to destroy us?” and threatened to take Leila’s younger sister out of school. Her mental health deteriorated further. She attempted suicide, was revived in an ambulance and spent three days in intensive care.
> 
> Afterwards, her father promised to fund her transition if she came to Jordan to see a therapist, and Leila obliged. The therapist, however, turned out to be a religious bigot, and the doctors she encountered in Jordan treated her with contempt, referring to her as “the spectacle” and diagnosing her with “Gender Identity Disorder.” Doctors asked her why she would want to be a woman when women were “inferior”, and asked her: “God blessed men with penises, so why would you want to deform yourself?”
> 
> Leila was medicated against her will with valproic acid (used for epilepsy patients), which caused her liver complications. She was forced to detransition and to cut her hair. When she attempted suicide again, she was left unable to walk for two weeks.
> 
> Leila returned to Turkey and was forced to stay in her family’s house. Soon afterwards, however, she checked herself into a mental hospital again because, in her words, it was the only place where she felt she had dignity.
> 
> It was around this time that I visited her in Turkey. She was full of levity and grace, and the nurses adored her. I had no idea what she had just been through because she was so charming and vibrant – the whole time I was with her she just joked and laughed. It was only later that I discovered the extent of her trauma, when she casually sent me a GoFundMe explaining what had happened to her, which I now cherish as an archive of her story.
> 
> Despite everything she had endured, she continued building a life. Around this time, in 2022 – despite it having been the worst year of her life – she graduated from Bahçeşehir University with a Bachelor of Science in Software Engineering. She later received a Master of Science from the same university.
> 
> Eventually, Leila ran away from home again in Turkey, and in November 2023, she sought asylum in Argentina.
> 
> Building a Life as an Activist
> Despite feeling lonely at first, Leila established herself in Argentina within a loving community of friends who celebrated her for who she was. She soon began transforming her own experiences of displacement and exclusion into a source of support for others, and became executive director of a trans organization called Trans-IT, where she worked to secure jobs and create economic opportunities for trans refugees and Latin Americans.
> 
> 
> 
> As part of Trans-IT, she visited embassies, banks and international organisations across Argentina, connecting trans migrants and trans Latin Americans to employment and helping them build stable livelihoods. She held events to help trans people in Latin America become entrepreneurs, secure financially empowered futures and achieve greater financial independence. She held coding workshops for trans migrants seeking to enter the technology sector, and trained employers on creating more inclusive workplaces.
> 
> Through Trans-IT, she created eight-week programmes for trans women in Latin America to enter remote work via the freelancing platform Upwork. She matched participants with jobs and mentored them afterwards to help them remain financially stable. Because of her advocacy work with Trans-IT, community members holding temporary refugee status were able to open bank accounts at Santander Bank – a significant step for migrants who had struggled to access basic financial services. Leila transformed the trauma of being houseless and financially insecure, as a Syrian refugee trans woman who faced rejection from her family and larger forces, into healing for other trans people in similar situations.
> 
> She was also involved in efforts to change policies affecting refugees in Argentina. At a remarkably young age, Leila became a board member of Derechos Humanos y Diversidad, an LGBTQIA+ organisation providing services to Latin American and Caribbean asylum seekers.
> 
> 
> 
> She also worked with Marginalized Majority, an organisation for queer people of the Global South. It was through this work that I first met Leila: we were the only Syrians in a remote support group held for queer Arabs during the first years of the pandemic. I immediately gravitated toward her laughter and we began to talk almost everyday. It was so healing to talk about our shared struggles and hopes for revolution in Syria so often misunderstood by others. When Leila became a lead community coordinator at Marginalized Majority, she held weekly drop-in spaces for queer and trans communities, organised seminars (including ones she invited me to speak at), and created spaces where people could talk openly about their experiences as queer people from the Global South.
> 
> One participant remembered her this way: “She was just always so cheerful and welcoming to me and to everyone during the drop-in days, and she wasn’t afraid to speak her mind or to talk about her story, and she wrote so beautifully it always moved me to tears.”
> 
> 
> 
> Fluent in Arabic, English, Turkish, and Spanish as a result of her many displacements, Leila held these drop-ins for nearly three years, almost never missing a weekend or a session. Her mentor, Jad Jaber remembers her as “a guide, a healer to the whole community.”
> 
> Leila had a huge tattoo of a phoenix over her self-harm scars because it symbolized how she revived herself from the ashes, over and over. For all the things Leila had survived – including, for the next few years, serious issues in her pancreas, which she had multiple surgeries for – this was perhaps what defined her most: she did not allow survival to become the limit of her life.
> 
> 
> 
> She was so deeply passionate about her work that she would refuse to take time off to rest and would power through, holding events for the trans community and fundraising for Trans-IT even when she was in and out of treatments in Argentina. I regularly heard her health updates and was grateful she made it through – and, when she passed away, she had been in remission since November of 2025 and seemed to be on the mend.
> 
> 
> 
> Who Gets to Remember Leila?
> When the Assad regime fell, I visited Syria briefly. Leila sent me the coordinates of the home in Jobar that her family had fled, and asked me to visit it. I took a video for her, and she said it warmed her heart, and that she wished more than anything to visit.
> 
> In the last months of her life, she was finally able to return to Damascus. She drove around the country, volunteering and visiting places she had always wanted to see. She loved Syria more than she loved anything. She once told me, “I talk about Syria the way a sane person talks about a lover. I wake up in the morning and say sabah el-kheir [good morning] to Mount Qasyoun. And I go to water my jasmines, and talk to them.”
> 
> The last message she sent me was about how happy she was to be home, and that we would talk soon. I had hoped to see her in Syria, but my Syrian papers were delayed, along with those of 8 million other Syrians, and the regional war broke out. I wish now that I had seen my beautiful friend one last time.
> 
> Leila was found dead in her apartment in Damascus during the last week of June. The circumstances of her death remain unclear.
> 
> But her death has exposed another kind of violence: the struggle over who gets to define a person, and how they are remembered after they are gone. Leila is being remembered across Damascus by people who misunderstood her, and by people who call her by the name she fought so hard not to be called. Guardians of Equality, SyriaUntold, SEEN for Equity, and Daraj Media have all documented how trans women and queer people in post-Assad Syria have had to “compress” themselves to survive. They face high risks of imprisonment, harassment, and terror. And in their deaths, they are erased.
> 
> As Hasnaa Alojaili of SEEN for Equity writes in an upcoming piece: “The death of Syrian trans women is not the end of the violence enacted upon their bodies. Rather, it may mark the beginning of a new stage of negotiation and struggle over who has the right to define the body, name it, mourn it, bury it, and remember it. At the moment of death, the trans body does not exit relations of power; it enters another form of them.” Alojaili describes this as a struggle involving state sovereignty through documents and law, family sovereignty through blood kinship and legal claims over the body, religious sovereignty through ritual and recognition, and social sovereignty through dominant norms of belonging.
> 
> Leila’s death has made that struggle painfully concrete.
> 
> The same community that contributed to much of her pain has misremembered her, called her by her deadname, and turned away from the healing force she was, recognizing only the parts of her which fit the conventional ideas of a society that values male authority above everything.
> 
> I want to remember our friend as Leila: empathetic, divine, sarcastic, hilarious, vivid, stubborn, intelligent, and beautiful. I recently replayed some of the hundreds of voice notes we used to send each other. In one of them, she said: “If I’m walking in the street and I see someone in need and I do not help them, I have no dignity. It reflects on me.” That was Leila. She always cared for the most vulnerable, and held space for thousands of trans and queer people across the Global South who were suffering.
> 
> Jad Jaber, the founder of Marginalized Majority and Leila’s mentor writes: “Leila Sawan. That was her name and her personhood. Beautiful and powerful. Say her name because her name echoes the eternal story of rising like a phoenix from the ashes. Her name is a prayer of hope, to all those who had to endure as much as she did… and yet achieved so much and actively made the world better than the world that was given to her. She is forever loved. She is forever love itself.”
> 
> Like Sarah Hegazi, Leila’s life cannot be separated from the violence which shaped her life and her struggle. Leila survived displacement, family rejection, institutional abuse, and illness. But, also like Sarah, I want to remember Leila for the life she built, not only for what happened to her. She built communities wherever she went. She helped other refugees find work, helped trans people access financial services, and spent years making other people feel less alone. She loved Syria enough to return to it.
> 
> That is how I want to remember her.
> 
> We will miss you, Leila, and we want justice for you. I hope there are jasmines and phoenixes in heaven.
