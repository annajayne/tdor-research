> "Coco always played the main role in her film"
> Konzert Theater Bern is dedicating a musical to Switzerland's most famous transgender person. Coco took her own life twenty years ago.
> Michael Feller
> Published: April 19, 2018, 12:59 PM
> 
> The journey back in time to Coco begins in the Sisters of Scissors hair salon in Bern's Lorraine district. Jean Cotter, hairdresser and makeup artist on Swiss film sets, was a companion of Coco – the woman who became Switzerland's most famous transgender person through Paul Riniker's documentary "Traum Frau Coco" (1991). A woman who spent her short life searching for the leading role.
> 
> Cotter lived with her in a shared apartment, performed with her in drag shows – and left after a falling out. "Her insane ego was unbearable," says Cotter. He never truly reconciled with Coco, who took her own life twenty years ago. "The pitcher goes to the well until it breaks. What's the point of mending the broken pieces then?"
> 
> Bird of paradise in Thun
> Coco was born Marc-Patric Lorétan in Thun in 1969, into a middle-class life: his father, mother, sister, and himself. But the small town was too confining a nest for nonconformists like him. Lorétan was the maverick of the high school. His unconventional clothing and his playful exploration of gender roles caused a sensation. He graduated in 1988. Or perhaps she did: By then, Lorétan was living as a woman in Bern. A beautiful woman who turned heads wherever she went.
> 
> Bern in the late 1980s was a fertile ground for people outside the norm. The Reitschule (riding school) was conquered, then the Dampfzentrale (steam center). With acid house, one of the first techno movements, a new music emerged, along with a new feeling of life. Euphoria, synthetic sounds, celebrated artificiality: attributes perfectly suited to the then-vibrant queer scene, for gay men, lesbians, and intersex people.
> 
> Right in the thick of one of the first acid house parties was this young person, whose male chromosomes were only apparent upon closer inspection. In December 1989, Coco, dressed in suspenders and a nun's habit, posed in the unheated Dampfzentrale club, alongside other men and women in go-go dancer attire. Jean Cotter was among them.
> 
> The complicated gay man
> Cotter remembers how he met Coco. He worked at the Ops Divina hair salon. Marc-Patric, also known as Coco, became his customer. Later, Cotter opened his own salon, FHK (for "free hair culture"), in Brunngasse – behind the Condomeria where Coco worked.
> 
> Soon the two were performing and became well-known throughout the city for their dance shows at parties in the riding school, the Dampfzentrale (a former steam-powered cultural center), and gay clubs, not least because they would strip naked without hesitation. Soon, a third member joined them: Carmila Wyss, who later called herself Pierce. "Der Die Das," as they called themselves, was the most exciting go-go group far and wide. "That was an intense time," recalls Jean Cotter.
> 
> Coco turned her playful exploration of gender roles into a serious one. At just 21, she underwent gender reassignment surgery, adapting her body to the feeling that a woman lived within this male shell.
> 
> Famous through a television documentary
> She was accompanied by a camera crew. Paul Riniker produced a documentary about her, filming before and after her surgeries. "Dream Woman Coco," broadcast on Swiss television in 1991, made the young transgender woman famous throughout Switzerland. "Dream Woman Coco" portrays a woman searching for her identity, perfectly embodying her role as a woman. Not for a second does the woman appear contrived; the man is barely perceptible. Her delicate features contribute to this. But so does her self-destructive drive to starve herself to an extreme thinness. A dark portrait in which an introspective, vulnerable Coco plays the leading role.
> 
> And one woman who isn't any happier with the gender reassignment: In the documentary, she puts it bluntly: "I've tinkered with the TV so much, but the film is still just so bad." A scene at the family table in Thun is almost unbearable. Coco's mother says of her 20-year-old child's decision to transition from biological son to daughter: "I think it's just great." Maintaining appearances is everything. Her other daughter, Dominique, died in 1989. She frequented the Platzspitz in Zurich.
> 
> With new «Püppi» in the Olmo
> The move to television was a step into public awareness. Suddenly everyone knew that such a thing existed, in Bern, in Thun, in the villages: people who are trapped in the wrong body. And who are fighting for normalcy.
> 
> When she went out in public, Coco remained exuberant and sought the limelight. Back then, Bern's subcultures frequented the Olmo clothing store on Zeughausgasse in Bern, co-founded by Francis Foss Pauchard. Pauchard retired a year and a half ago and handed the store over to his younger brothers. He remembers Coco well, her delicate features and her "grandeur." "It's actually astonishing how vividly she's remained in my memory," he says. "Extroverted people have always been good for this city." He saw all sorts of oddballs in his store. He recalls the brothers Stevo and Nilo Nilovic, who now run the restaurants Luce and Il Grissino. They were known as Teddys in the early 80s, when rock 'n' roll and the iconic Elvis pompadour were experiencing a revival. A few years later, Coco arrived.
> 
> A model with taste
> "It was always a bit of a spectacle when she came over; she was incredibly attention-seeking." The combination of her beauty and self-promotion piqued his interest. In the 80s, Pauchard played in the electropop duo Starter, one of the first in Switzerland to utilize synthesizers and drum machines in their music. He drew inspiration for one song from his most striking client: "Coco Wears a Sad Face" was the title. The nickname was born. That was before Coco had gender reassignment surgery.
> 
> Later, Coco appeared as an Olmo model, a role tailor-made for her. "She not only had charisma but also impeccable taste," says Pauchard. She made a grand appearance at a fashion event in the Stufenbau Ittigen. "She always played the leading role in her own film." Sometimes, Pauchard felt Coco's extroversion went too far. "After her gender reassignment surgery, she came to Olmo and showed me her new 'dolls' in the shop."
> 
> Drag queens, hip-hoppers, pigs
> From 1994, Coco lived in the "drag house" in Obstberg, together with Jean Cotter and Carmila Wyss, her two comrades from "Der Die Das." Here, the three of them had their own stage. Wild parties took place in the squat. Jean Cotter recalls: "The house was big, but full of junk. With our exhibitions, events, and parties, we financed the next skip load of rubbish to clear out another room." A house pig lived under the stairs. Everyone who recounts the days of the raucous and intoxicating parties in the drag house remembers the pig.
> 
> Veronika Minder also remembers the pig. The now 70-year-old former record store owner, cinema operator, filmmaker, and author probably met Coco "at the Drei Eidgenossen" (Three Swiss Confederates) pub, where Cotter and Carmila also frequented. "I really liked Coco," says Minder, who has always been interested in and championed outsiders.
> 
> One party at the Tuntenhaus (a former drag club) remains particularly vivid in her memory. Her son Baldy, the future Wurzel 5 manager, then 17 years old, performed with his rap crew. At that time, Biel was the epicenter of the hip-hop scene. All the Biel hip-hoppers traveled to Bern for the concert. "Their jaws dropped when they saw all the men in women's clothing."
> 
> Superstar in Paris
> Veronika Minder coached Coco for a while, when Riniker's film "Dream Woman Coco" was gaining international attention. The two traveled to Vienna and Paris, where Coco was celebrated like a star by the local gay, lesbian, and trans communities. "She had incredible 'screams,'" says Minder, "Kim Kardashian would have paled in comparison."
> 
> "Coco had incredible 'screams', Kim Kardashian would have paled in comparison."
> 
> Veronika Minder
> Coco told the Viennese about her macrobiotic recipes; "she had a missionary zeal," says Minder. The young transgender people hung on the words of the self-assured woman from Thun. In Paris, she partied at the legendary Palace nightclub. Minder is convinced that Coco would be an influencer today, marketing herself via social media like Instagram and living a good life. "Even 'Der Die Das' could have made it big," Minder believes.
> 
> But the Coco era was short-lived. Claudine-Eve Loretan, as she was officially called after the operation, suffered from the consequences of gender reassignment and the side effects of the medication she had to take. Her mental health remained unstable. To earn money, she worked as a prostitute in brothels in Bern and Thun. There, she still attracted attention.
> 
> Gruesome suicide
> Longtime friends turned their backs on her. Jean Cotter, and later Carmila Wyss. They lived together in Obstberg for two years, then Cotter couldn't take it anymore. "For a while, we were like a surrogate family to her." But Coco's inner turmoil put a strain on the group. On the one hand, there was her need for recognition, her extravagant lifestyle, her obsession with healthy eating, and her excessive cocaine use. On the other hand, Coco struggled with life. Her longing for normalcy, for a completely ordinary man, was incompatible with her reality.
> 
> On September 25, 1998, almost two years after the trio had broken up, Coco took her own life. She had found refuge with a friend, a single mother with two children. When the woman came home one day, she found Coco hanged.
> 
> An abrupt break in the film, yet simultaneously an uncompromising production à la Coco, right to the very end. Even today, Jean Cotter has no understanding for the gruesome suicide. What's the point of mending the pieces after that?




> Eve-Claudine Lorétan (* 1969 ; † 1998 ), known by the stage name Coco , was a Swiss model and the first prominent transgender Swiss woman.
> 
> Life
> Lorétan was born Marc-Patric Lorétan in Thun and graduated from the Seefeld Gymnasium while training as a model under the name "Coco". In 1990, she decided to undergo gender reassignment surgery and was legally recognized as a woman from 1992 onwards.
> 
> The operation was documented by Paul Riniker in his documentary film *Traum Frau Coco* (Dream Woman Coco) . In 1991, an exceptionally large number of viewers – 660,000 people – watched the film on Swiss television . Coco, and with her the topic of transgender identity, became known nationwide. [ 1 ]
> 
> The film brought Coco widespread popularity and initially boosted her modeling career. However, her physical and mental health deteriorated rapidly. She became depressed and psychotic and suffered from osteoporosis as a result of gender reassignment surgery . In 1997, the manuscript of her autobiography was stolen, and Coco began working as a prostitute out of financial desperation. In the autumn of 1998, she took her own life at the age of 29. [ 2 ] [ 3 ]
> 
> reception
> Besides the film Dream Woman Coco (1991), Coco's life is also the subject of the musical Coco – A Transgender Musical (2018) by author Alexander Seibt and musician Markus Schönholzer. In the show, Coco is played by Mariananda Schempp, and her male body by Gabriel Schneider. [ 4 ]
> 
> In 2019, photographer Olivier G. Fatton published the photo book "Coco". It contains images from the five years Fatton was associated with Coco and a biographical text by Dunia Miralles. [ 5 ]




> Series Part 2: Transgender people in Switzerland
> Coco was not of this world
> Twenty years ago, the first known transgender woman in Switzerland died. Now her life story is being brought to the stage.
> Published: January 22, 2018 at 7:58 PM
> 
> In 1990, Marc-Patric became Eve-Claudine Lorétan, who thrilled the Bernese party scene under the stage name Coco.
> 
> Eve-Claudine Lorétan alias Coco was born Marc-Patric Lorétan in 1969 and grew up in Thun.
> 
> Documentary filmmaker Paul Riniker (second from right) and Eve-Claudine «Coco» Lorétan (left of Riniker) at the Tele award ceremony for the film «Traum Frau Coco».
> 1/4
> 
> In 1990, Marc-Patric became Eve-Claudine Lorétan, who thrilled the Bernese party scene under the stage name Coco.
> 
> In Bern, it's already the talk of the town: the premiere of the "transgender musical Coco" on April 20th at the Vidmar stage of the venerable city theater. Twenty years ago, in 1998, Eve-Claudine Lorétan, also known as Coco, took her own life; she was the first known transgender woman in Switzerland. "The musical makes no claim to historical accuracy; instead, it is a long-overdue tribute to a woman who pursued her challenging path with determination and dignity," say the program organizers.
> 
> Coco was born Marc-Patric Lorétan in 1969. Her childhood in the stifling 1970s in conservative Thun was very difficult. "I was a child prodigy, but in the wrong body," she said in an interview. Classmates recall: "She was like a mythical creature, not of this world." The more open spirit of the 1980s was more to her liking. In 1987, Marc-Patric trained as a model under the stage name Coco. He graduated from high school in 1988, and in 1990 underwent gender reassignment surgery, becoming Claudine-Eve. But her mother, Trudy, later said in an interview with BLICK: "She was never truly happy. Life broke her." After the surgeries, she suffered from depression and a severe identity crisis. Doctors also diagnosed her with psychosis.
> 
> A piece of Swiss media history
> In 1991, Coco made Swiss media history and became a household name nationwide. Paul Riniker (72) broadcast his acclaimed documentary "Dream Woman Coco" on SRF, which attracted 660,000 viewers (see sidebar). In 1992, Coco was officially recognized as a woman, and her passport now read Eve-Claudine Lorétan. She enjoyed star status in social circles, worked as a model, and was a welcome guest everywhere. However, her health deteriorated. She began to suffer from osteoporosis, apparently a consequence of her gender reassignment. "If I had remained a man," she told BLICK in 1995, "I wouldn't be ill now. I fear winter most of all. The cold intensifies my pain. My wrists hurt, my back, and, worst of all, my hip." She began to switch to a macrobiotic diet and trained as a chef.
> 
> Suicide in autumn 1998 
> In 1997, another tragedy struck. Thieves stole the 1,000-page manuscript of her autobiography. At the same time, it became public that, driven by financial desperation, she was working as a prostitute in several establishments in Thun. In September 1998, the situation became critical. "She had barely eaten or drunk anything recently," her mother told BLICK. On the phone, her mother had pleaded with her daughter to come home. "Together we'll manage!" But Coco was too weak and said she couldn't even get out of bed. "Mommy, let me go! I can't go on." The next day, she hanged herself. "How desperate she must have been..." Her mother was always convinced: "Coco may have had some surgery to remove some of her body parts, but that never made her a woman."
> 
> Documentary filmmaker Paul Riniker remembers
> For 18 months, Paul Riniker (72) followed the story of Coco, who underwent gender reassignment surgery during filming. The film aired in October 1991 and was a ratings hit: “My memories are mixed. It was fascinating to engage with the intelligent, but also impulsive and occasionally irritating Coco. In retrospect, it distresses me to realize that she was already very self-destructive back then, as if she wasn't allowed to be happy. Knowing that she later took her own life, it seems to me as if her end had been predetermined long before. The making of the film was one thing, what happened after the broadcast was another. Hardly any TV program of those years triggered such a strong and lasting response. And Coco deeply impressed the audience.” The ending was particularly dramatic. Riniker's last question to Coco: “Would you have the surgery again?” Her answer: “No, I'd rather kill myself.”
> 
> "The film still makes sense to me."
> Riniker would still make the film in a similar way. "For me, it still feels right. But so much has changed in the decades since. The transgender issue has created a large community that rightly makes its voice heard and asserts its demands. For example, for more appropriate language. Today, we no longer say 'transformation' but 'adaptation' when the body is surgically aligned with one's gender identity. What has also completely changed is the awareness of a third gender. We now know that tens of thousands of people are neither clearly female nor clearly male, but that there are many forms of mixed gender. And these days, every newborn is no longer so readily surgically assigned a gender."
